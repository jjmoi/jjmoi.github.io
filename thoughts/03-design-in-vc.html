Design in VC
250420

Topics
Venture Capital

I did not plan to end up in venture capital. In fact, almost no one can plan for it, unless you are rich enough to start your own fund. Breaking into VC is largely relationship-driven. You get there because someone with access and capital believes you should be in the room. Design Partner is one of the rarest roles in venture capital, sitting somewhere between design and investing.

Before joining venture, I was Head of Design at a successful unicorn startup that almost nobody had heard of because the work lived in unsexy domains: biotech, government, defense, materials, all the boring things. If I was doing it right, the work should not make a sound. It should just quietly mint money.

The company got acquired for billions of dollars. The founders moved on to start venture funds and pulled me in as a founding partner. It was luck, but it was also earned. They had already seen how I worked.

A Design Partner in VC is not really a design role. It is a hybrid of investor, analyst, recruiter, consultant, and only occasionally designer. That occasional part is arguably the most important. Technology can make products people need. Design can make them products people desire.

Design should sit at the same level as technology. Technology creates possibilities. Design turns them into something real, something people can use and something people can believe in.

One unusual thing about design in venture is how far upstream you get to work. Instead of being brought in after the product has already taken shape, you can be there before the company, the product, or even the founding team fully exists. That changes the role completely.

So what did I actually do day to day? I worked as far upstream as design can go. I performed due diligence on thousands of potential investments a year, focusing on the design aspects of the business. I helped portfolio companies build their first prototypes. I made a lot of logos and an absurd number of pitch decks. Like a design consultant, I helped founders sharpen their story, clarify what they were building, and shape the narrative and positioning around it. A good story buys you time while you are still searching for product-market fit. Then I helped translate that story into product reality. The first user. The first workflow. The first proof of concept. The first bit of culture. The first t-shirt.

I also incubated several startups myself, then went out to find the founders to run them. In a way, I was the founder before we found the official founder. That gave me access to projects I would never have touched otherwise: a nuclear power plant, a hotel in space, a floating city for the Waterworld timeline. It felt like running a venture design studio with unusually wide latitude. I could choose the problems that interested me most without having to chase consulting clients or cash flow. The economics were different too. Not every company had to work. A small number of breakout companies could carry the portfolio.

Sometimes there were also curveball requests from investors, like designing a logo for their daughter's coffee shop. Oh well.

I am still most interested in the same moment: before the company is obvious, before the category exists, and before anyone knows exactly what the product should become. Pre-hype. Pre-seed. If you are building frontier technology, or just something genuinely strange and ambitious, we should talk.
