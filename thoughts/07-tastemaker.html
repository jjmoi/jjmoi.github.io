A designer asks an AI system for twenty versions of a settings page. A few seconds later, they got what they asked for. Some are better than others, most are fine, and that’s the problem. For the longest time, design was difficult enough that difficulty itself acted as a filter. Many bad ideas disappeared because nobody had the time to finish them and AI changes that for the profession. Now that the bad ideas can be built cheaply, deciding whether the design option is worth anything at all becomes the expense.

Once described by Ira Glass, a common problem among creatives is that we often have good taste long before we have the actual skill to produce work that meets our own taste. We can recognize the gap between what we admire and what we are capable of making, and the only way through it was simply to make a great deal of work until ability began to catch up with taste.

AI can close much of the execution gap almost immediately. Anyone with little experience can produce something appeared polished on the first day, but the taste gap has not disappeared. Without years of experience, you can’t tell the truly good idea out of 20 slightly worse versions sitting in front of you. Worse, the totally wrong ideas that aren’t obvious.

This ability has many names; taste, vibe, craft, sense, judegment. The word can sound precious and vauge, but the underlying skill is practical. It is knowing that a design might feel good at first but irritating after a little while or knowing when to break consistency. To be content with AI, a designer has to gone through as many bad decisions as they generate the volume of work with AI. The designer has to know what to look for, why one option is stronger than another and when all twenty should be rejected. The role shifts from producing every answer to maintaining the standard by which answers are judged. A good designer can now explore thirty directions instead of three, build working prototypes instead of static mockups and examine edge cases before engineering commits to a feature. That is real leverage. But it only matters if the designer can distinguish useful exploration from thirty variations of the same mediocre idea. The same problem appears in design systems. A component library can tell AI what a button looks like. It cannot tell AI whether the button should be there. That requires rules about behavior, examples, exceptions, language, product principles and explanations of when not to use something. The system has to contain some of the judgment that previously lived inside the heads of experienced designers. As AI gets better at execution, polish will become less meaningful as evidence of skill. Almost anyone will be able to make something that looks finished. The harder skill will be recognizing what deserves to be finished in the first place.

The mistake most founders make is chasing prestige. The designer you’re actually looking for is a true generalist with taste and judgment. So, look for range. A strong founding designer is part product strategist, part storyteller, part craftsperson. They know how to run customer interviews, build the prototype, sweat the details, and build a pitch deck for investors at night, while still be able to step back at any point to ask if the whole thing even makes sense. Think more Da Vinci than feature factory. They thrive in ambiguity, love to navigate the fog, balance speed with clarity, and care about the essence of the product, not just its surface.

They’re not gonna be hanging out in obvious places. Look for freelancers who’ve quietly built entire products from scratch. Look for the first designers at small teams doing the heavy lifting. Look for people solving problems, not chasing clout. Follow the trail of products you admire back to the people that shaped them.

When you do meet one, run a process that respects their craft. Skip the whiteboard puzzles because it’s imppossible to actually assess the quality anyway. Instead, bring them your real mess and the magic, and see how they think, how they question, how they balance vision within constraint. The right designer will push back on your ideas, refine it, and oftentimes dismantle it so it can be rebuilt stronger.

These design people want to work on things that matter. They want ownership, autonomy, and founders who treats design as a core discipline. A designer can tell immediately if you see them as equal partner or an accessory. Give them that, give them a glorious purpose, in order to outplay big tech. Show them design is part of your company’s DNA. Write a job description that speaks like a human with empathy and wit and creativity. Share your philosophy, your conviction that design is central, not cosmetic. Philosophers from Aristotle to Taoists understood, form and essence are inseparable. That’s what great designers bring to your company.

Hiring your first designer isn’t about chasing a unicorn or a pegasus. It’s about clarity in finding someone whose craft, ambition, and philosophy align with yours. And you do that by treating design as foundational and you’ll attract the ones who want to build the thing you actually need, a product people love to use.
